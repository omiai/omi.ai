A Guide for International Readers

How Japan's Marriage Agencies Work

In Japan, a marriage can be built in two entirely different ways. This is a guide to the second one — the contract marriage: a partnership constructed deliberately, by agreement and process, rather than grown out of romance.

Start Reading 結婚相談所

I. The Premise

Not a love marriage — a contract marriage

In much of the world, there is one accepted way for a marriage to come into being: romance. You meet someone by chance, feelings develop, and the relationship follows the feelings — if all goes well, into marriage. That is a love marriage, and Japan has it too.

But Japan also maintains a second, entirely separate way of building a marriage: the marriage agency (結婚相談所, kekkon soudanjo). What the agency produces is a contract marriage — and the essential thing to understand is that this is not a love marriage arranged more efficiently. It is a different method of constructing a human relationship altogether. A love marriage lets emotion lead and the relationship follow. A contract marriage reverses this: the commitment to marry comes first, as an explicit mutual agreement, and the partnership is then built deliberately — on agreed rules, honest disclosure, compatibility, and mutual respect.

A love marriage builds a relationship on feelings that arrived by chance. A contract marriage builds it by deliberate agreement. These are two different ways of constructing a human relationship — not two versions of the same one.

The key premise

Everyone who joins a marriage agency has already decided one thing before meeting anyone: they intend to marry. What the system provides is a structured, rules-based process for two such people to find each other, verify their compatibility, and reach an engagement — typically within months, not years.

Two ways of building a marriage
Love marriage Contract marriage
Starts from A chance meeting and emotions A deliberate decision to marry
The relationship is Wherever the feelings lead it Constructed step by step through an agreed process
Commitment Arrives last, if at all Declared first, before anyone meets
Foundation Romantic feeling Agreement, honest disclosure, compatibility, and mutual respect

This is the modern descendant of Japan's traditional omiai (arranged introduction) culture. It is not a lesser form of marriage, and it is not an unfinished love marriage waiting for romance to arrive. It is judged by its own standard: whether two people can honestly agree on, and then build, a stable partnership for life.

Most agencies in Japan belong to large nationwide matchmaking networks that share a common member database and a common rulebook. The process described below follows the standard system used across one of Japan's largest such networks, so what you read here reflects how the mainstream of this industry actually operates.

II. The Path

Four stages, from first meeting to engagement

The journey is divided into clearly named stages. Every member knows which stage they are in with each person, and the rules of each stage are understood by everyone. There is no ambiguity of the "so… what are we?" kind.

  1. お見合い

    Omiai — the first meeting

    omiai

    Members browse detailed, verified profiles and request meetings. If both sides accept, an omiai is arranged: a first meeting of about one hour, typically over tea or coffee in a hotel lounge. It is polite, low-pressure, and has one purpose — to see whether you would both like to meet again.

    Afterwards, each person independently tells the agency yes or no. Only if both say yes does the relationship move forward. A "no" is delivered through the agency, so nobody has to reject anyone face-to-face.

  2. プレ交際

    Pre-Kousai — trial dating

    pre-kousai; sometimes kari-kousai (仮交際)

    When both people want to continue after the omiai, they enter trial dating. They exchange contact details and go on ordinary dates — meals, walks, day trips — to find out whether the compatibility is real.

    Important: during this stage, each person may be in trial dating with several people at the same time. This is fully within the rules, everyone in the system knows it, and it is not considered cheating. This point surprises many readers from other countries, so the next section explains it properly.

  3. 真剣交際

    Shinken Kousai — exclusive, serious dating

    shinken kousai

    When one connection stands out, the couple agrees to enter serious dating. At this moment, both people end all of their other trial relationships and stop accepting new omiai requests. From here on, the relationship is strictly one-to-one and exclusive.

    This is the stage where the couple talks concretely about married life: living arrangements, work, finances, family, children. The goal is to reach a confident mutual decision about marriage, usually within a few months.

  4. 婚約成立

    Engagement — the goal of the process

    kon'yaku seiritsu; called seikon (成婚) in agency terms

    When both partners are sure, there is a proposal and the couple becomes engaged. In agency terminology this is called seikon — "successful marriage outcome" — and it marks the completion of the agency process. The couple leaves the agency and proceeds to their wedding as any engaged couple would.

III. The Misunderstood Part

Why dating several people at once is not cheating here

If you come from a dating culture where seeing multiple people at the same time is a betrayal, the Pre-Kousai stage can look shocking at first. We want to explain this honestly and directly, because it is the single most misunderstood part of the system.

1. It is an official rule that everyone has agreed to

Concurrent trial dating is not a loophole or something people do behind each other's backs. It is written into the system's rules, explained to every member when they join, and understood by every person you will meet. When two people begin Pre-Kousai, both of them know the other may also be seeing other candidates. There is no deception — and cheating, at its core, is a breach of trust through deception. Where there is no deception and no violated agreement, there is no cheating.

2. Trial dating is not yet a relationship

In this community, Pre-Kousai is understood as a compatibility-testing period, not as being boyfriend and girlfriend. The commitment of exclusivity has simply not been made yet — by anyone, to anyone. It is closer in spirit to a series of respectful interviews for the most important partnership of your life than to a romance already in progress.

3. Exclusivity has a clear, formal beginning

What makes this workable is that the system draws a bright, unmistakable line: the moment a couple enters Shinken Kousai (serious dating), exclusivity begins — completely. All other trial relationships are formally ended, and seeing anyone else from that point on would be a genuine violation, treated seriously by the agency. So it is not that this culture lacks fidelity; it is that fidelity begins at a clearly agreed moment, rather than at an ambiguous, unspoken one.

A cultural comparison

It may help to see that cultures differ mainly in when exclusivity is assumed to start — not in whether it matters:

When does exclusivity begin?
Typical Western dating Japanese marriage agency system
Early stage "Casually dating" — seeing multiple people is often accepted, but the boundaries are unspoken and vary by person Pre-Kousai — seeing multiple people is an explicit, universal rule known to all
Exclusivity begins Negotiated informally ("the talk"); timing varies and is a common source of misunderstanding At the formal transition to Shinken Kousai — a defined moment both partners declare
After exclusivity Seeing others is cheating Seeing others is cheating — and a violation of agency rules
Endpoint Open-ended; marriage may or may not be the goal Marriage is the declared goal for every participant from day one

Seen this way, the agency system is arguably more transparent than informal dating: every participant knows exactly what stage they are in, what the other person may be doing, and what has and has not been promised. The rules protect everyone's honesty rather than undermining it.

In short

Within Japan's marriage-agency community, dating several people during Pre-Kousai is honest participation in an agreed process. Cheating, in the true sense, only becomes possible once exclusivity has been formally promised — and at that point, the system condemns it as firmly as any culture does.

IV. The Support

You are not doing this alone

A final feature worth knowing: throughout all of these stages, each member is supported by a professional matchmaker or counselor at their agency. The matchmaker arranges the omiai, relays each side's intentions after meetings, advises members between dates, and helps navigate the transitions between stages. Difficult messages — declining another meeting, ending a trial relationship — are passed through the agency, which keeps the process courteous and spares both sides unnecessary hurt.

This support structure is a large part of why the system works: honest communication is built into the machinery, rather than left to chance and courage.

V. In Summary

The system at a glance

  • Japan's marriage agencies produce contract marriages — a fundamentally different method of building a human relationship from love marriage. Commitment is declared first, and the partnership is constructed deliberately on agreement, honesty, and mutual respect.
  • The process moves through four defined stages: Omiai → Pre-Kousai → Shinken Kousai → Engagement.
  • During Pre-Kousai, dating several candidates at once is an explicit rule that all members have agreed to. Because there is no deception and no broken promise, it is not cheating within this community.
  • Exclusivity begins at Shinken Kousai — a formal, mutual declaration — and from that point the couple is fully committed to each other.
  • The process is designed for clarity, honesty, and respect at every step, with professional support throughout.
